# Research Paper Writing Style Guide

This is our working guide for writing a clear, natural and publication-ready
research paper. It combines the three uploaded workflows (Experimental Paper
Blueprint, AI Q1 Paper Architect and AI Peer Reviewer Bot) with current
publication guidance from the target journal, Elsevier and ICMJE.

This guide is for improving clarity, coherence and research integrity. It is
not a method for bypassing AI detectors or concealing authorship. The human
authors remain responsible for every claim, analysis, citation and decision.

## 1. Default writing style

- Write in natural academic English with a calm, precise voice.
- Prefer concrete nouns and active verbs over inflated adjectives.
- Vary sentence length and paragraph rhythm naturally; do not force variation.
- Use transitions only when they show a real logical relationship.
- Avoid repetitive sentence templates, repeated claims and synonym-swapping
  that changes wording without improving the reasoning.
- Keep technical terms, statistical notation and domain-specific meaning exact.
- Use cautious claims: distinguish association from causation and statistical
  significance from clinical or practical importance.
- Avoid first-person unless the target journal's model papers routinely use it.
- Do not use fake quotations, fabricated numbers, invented references or claims
  that cannot be traced to the data or a reliable source.

## 2. What natural academic prose should sound like

Good prose usually follows this sequence:

1. Make the claim or point.
2. Give the evidence, method or citation supporting it.
3. Explain why it matters for the research question.
4. Transition to the next point only when the relationship is clear.

Use a mix of short sentences for emphasis and longer sentences when a method or
relationship genuinely needs qualification. Keep paragraphs focused on one
idea. A paragraph should normally have a topic sentence, supporting evidence and
an interpretation or link to the next paragraph.

## 3. Formulaic wording to avoid

These phrases are not automatically wrong, but they often make a draft sound
generic when repeated or used without a specific purpose:

- "In today's rapidly changing world..."
- "It is important to note that..."
- "This study aims to..." in every section
- "Moreover, furthermore, in addition" at the start of many paragraphs
- "A plethora of studies..."
- "Harnessing the power of..."
- "Revolutionize" or "groundbreaking" without evidence
- "Needless to say..."
- "As can be clearly seen..."
- "The results clearly prove..." when the design supports only an association
- Repeated endings such as "This highlights the importance of..."

Replace a generic opening with the specific problem, population, dataset or
evidence. Do not remove a phrase merely because it appears on an avoidance list;
retain it when it is the most accurate wording.

## 4. Repetition control

- Track the main claim of each paragraph in an outline before drafting.
- State each result once in the Results section; interpret it in Discussion
  without copying the same sentence.
- Do not repeat the same sentence with different synonyms. Change the logic,
  evidence or level of detail instead.
- Use consistent terminology for the same variable, outcome and model.
- When a term must recur for precision, repeat the term rather than introducing
  misleading synonyms.
- During revision, search for repeated 5–8 word sequences and repeated paragraph
  openings.

## 5. Humanisation without loss of scientific precision

Humanised academic writing does not mean informal writing. It means that the
prose reflects a real line of reasoning:

- Start from the actual research problem, not a generic global statement.
- Explain why a methodological choice was made.
- Acknowledge uncertainty, boundary conditions and limitations.
- Connect findings to the people, systems or decisions affected by them.
- Use measured interpretation instead of promotional language.
- Preserve the author's legitimate disciplinary voice and local context.

Humanisation is not random synonym replacement, deliberate grammar errors or
adding personal anecdotes that do not serve the research.

## 6. Research and evidence skills required

### Journal fit and journal DNA

- Check aims, scope, article type, word limit and reporting requirements.
- Read 3–5 recent papers from the target journal.
- Record section order, contribution language, Results/Discussion balance,
  terminology, figure conventions and citation style.

### Research-gap and argument design

- Separate a lack of evidence, a methodological limitation, a controversy and
  a practical problem.
- State the research question before drafting the contribution.
- Ensure every objective is answered by a method and a result.
- Link each contribution to evidence in the paper.

### Paper architecture

- Use the IMRAD logic (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion) unless the
  journal or study design requires another structure.
- Build a section-level outline before writing paragraphs.
- Allocate a word budget from comparable papers, then adjust it to the journal.
- Add a separate literature review or theoretical framework only when the field
  and the argument require it.

### Methods and reproducibility

- Describe data source/version, sampling, eligibility, preprocessing, variables,
  analysis, software versions, hyperparameters and validation.
- Explain why a method was selected and state its assumptions.
- Prevent data leakage and separate prespecified from exploratory analyses.
- Report enough information for an independent researcher to reproduce the work.

### Statistics and data integrity

- Report effect sizes and uncertainty, not only P values.
- Give absolute numbers alongside percentages.
- Match each analysis to the research question and outcome type.
- Report missing data, class imbalance, calibration and subgroup performance when
  relevant.
- Distinguish statistical significance from clinical or practical significance.

### Results and visual communication

- Present findings in a logical order, with the primary result first.
- Use tables and figures to clarify the argument, not to decorate the paper.
- Avoid duplicating the same data in text, table and figure.
- Label axes, units, sample sizes, confidence intervals and abbreviations.
- Explain the message of each figure in the surrounding text.

### Discussion and interpretation

- Begin with the main finding, then compare it with relevant evidence.
- Explain plausible mechanisms without presenting speculation as fact.
- Discuss generalisability, bias, fairness, implementation and limitations.
- Keep conclusions proportional to the design and data.

### References and plagiarism control

- Cite the original study or dataset whenever possible.
- Verify each citation against the original article or a trusted index.
- Never cite AI output as the primary scientific source.
- Use quotation marks and page numbers for any direct quotation.
- Paraphrasing requires genuine rewriting from understanding plus a citation;
  replacing a few words is not sufficient.
- Maintain an evidence ledger: claim, source, exact support, page/section and
  whether the claim is original analysis or external evidence.
- Check for accidental text overlap, copied figure elements and retracted
  references before submission.

### Ethics, authorship and transparency

- Include ethics approval, consent or waiver, privacy safeguards and data access
  restrictions where applicable.
- List only human contributors who meet the journal's authorship criteria.
- Provide CRediT contributions, funding and competing-interest statements.
- Obtain permission for identifiable data, images, quotations and copyrighted
  material.

### Revision and peer-review readiness

- Run a scope and desk-rejection audit before language editing.
- Test the gap logic, methodological rigor, data integrity and journal fit.
- Make 5–10 high-impact language edits after the scientific structure is stable.
- Address one priority critique at a time and keep a response/change log.
- Ask a subject-matter expert or statistician to review high-risk decisions.

### LaTeX and file hygiene

- Use the target journal's class and bibliography style where available.
- Keep figures, tables, source files and references versioned and editable.
- Use LaTeX for equations and statistical notation.
- Compile and inspect the PDF; check cross-references, captions and citations.
- Keep a clean submission folder separate from analysis notebooks and drafts.

## 7. Integrated workflow for our project

### Phase A — Evidence and scope

1. Confirm the target journal and article type.
2. Collect 5–10 model papers for outlining, or 3–5 for a focused reviewer audit.
3. Build a journal profile and an evidence ledger.

### Phase B — Blueprint

1. Estimate comparable paper lengths excluding references.
2. Decide the section order used in the target field.
3. Set a journal-compliant word budget.
4. Define the purpose and key evidence for every section.

### Phase C — One-section-at-a-time drafting

1. Choose the section to build.
2. Confirm the exact topic, research question and available evidence.
3. Create an outline based on model-paper structure.
4. Draft in natural prose from verified evidence.
5. Check repetition, logic, citations and unsupported claims.

### Phase D — Critical review

Run the five audits from the reviewer workflow:

1. Desk-rejection risk
2. Gap logic and novelty
3. Methodological rigor and data integrity
4. Journal-DNA compliance
5. Precision and conciseness edits

### Phase E — Human verification

The authors verify every number, citation, interpretation, ethical statement,
author contribution and final change before submission.

## 8. Informatics and Health requirements to keep visible

For a Research Article, the current official guide states:

- Maximum 5,000 words
- Structured abstract of no more than 400 words
- Four abstract parts: Background, Methods, Findings, Interpretation
- Maximum 6 keywords
- Numbered sections and subsections
- Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion and Conclusions
- Ethics, funding, author contributions, competing interests and acknowledgments
- Mandatory Highlights: 3–5 bullets, maximum 85 characters each, in a separate
  editable file
- Figures/tables placed near relevant text with captions
- Superscript Arabic references in order of appearance

The journal also encourages an optional graphical abstract and requires authors
to disclose substantive generative-AI use in a declaration before the references.

## 9. Responsible AI-use rule

AI may help with brainstorming, outline building, language improvement and
pre-submission critique, but it must not replace scientific judgment, data
analysis, interpretation or authorship. The researcher must verify all output.
If AI makes substantive changes to manuscript wording or organisation, disclose
the tool, purpose and human review according to the target journal's policy.

Suggested declaration template:

> During the preparation of this work, the authors used [TOOL/SERVICE] in order
> to [PURPOSE]. After using this tool/service, the authors reviewed and edited
> the content as needed and take full responsibility for the content of the
> published article.

## 10. Final pre-submission checklist

- [ ] The paper is in scope and the article type is correct.
- [ ] The research question, gap, method and results align.
- [ ] Every major claim has data or a reliable citation.
- [ ] No citation, number, quotation or figure has been invented or copied.
- [ ] The abstract matches the main text and reports the key numerical findings.
- [ ] Methods are reproducible and data leakage has been checked.
- [ ] Results are not duplicated across text, tables and figures.
- [ ] Discussion does not overclaim causality or clinical benefit.
- [ ] Ethics, consent, funding, contributions and competing interests are present.
- [ ] AI use is disclosed when required.
- [ ] References are verified and no retracted paper is cited unintentionally.
- [ ] The final PDF, source files and supplementary files open correctly.
